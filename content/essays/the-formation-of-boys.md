# The Formation of Boys

As I'm writing this, my boy is 15. It's 2026. I'm nervous, and excited for him. He's awesome. He's into blacksmithing, Muay Thai, math, learning. He's kind and funny. He loves spending time with us, and I love spending time with him. It won't last, he'll change, I'll change, and the world will change. But those changes - who's driving them? What's driving them? This essay is me thnking out loud. Any place it comes across as 'certain' is a side effect of writing. The reality is I have nothing but questions about everything here.

Richard Reeves, now president of the American Institute for Boys and Men, has assembled some numbers on parts of life for men, and they are sobering. Women are substantially more likely to finish college; AIBM puts the four-year graduation rate at four-year institutions at 54 percent for female students and 43 percent for male students. Men account for most fatal injury deaths, including roughly seven in ten opioid overdose deaths and nearly four in five suicides. In 2023, the male suicide rate was roughly four times the female rate, and about 39,000 men died by suicide in this country, a scale roughly comparable to annual U.S. deaths from breast cancer. In fall 2020, the first pandemic fall, male college enrollment fell 5.1 percent while female enrollment fell 0.7 percent. We've got the 'manosphere' hijacking the conversation with real data and bad faith. 

The trouble is broader than boys or men. It feels like another facet of the modernity issues we've been struggling with for a good while now: a modern world that has cultivated a particular kind of person, more individuated, more abstract, more cut off from place, body, lineage, and shared meaning. Boys are where some of the damage shows early and plainly. It's impacting all of us, but for this essay, let's focus on boys - on my son, on me.

Patrick Deneen, in *Why Liberalism Failed*, gives the political version of this. A civilization organized around maximizing individual choice will, over time, dissolve the families, communities, and traditions that produced capable individuals in the first place. The unencumbered self, freed from every bond it did not personally choose, eventually finds that the surrounding structures which used to hold a life together have thinned to nothing. Deneen himself has drifted toward a politics that I do not share. The book does not require it. The core observation stands on its own. We have spent a long time treating inherited obligation as a constraint to be removed, and we are living among the consequences of removing so much of it.

What we removed, in the case of boys, was an inheritance thousands of generations deep. For almost all of human history, becoming a man was not left to chance. Cultures carried boys across the gap with deliberate machinery: the slow work by which a child is turned into an adult of a particular kind. Joseph Campbell spent his life documenting how traditional societies moved boys through a structured passage--separation from childhood, ordeal, and return as someone with a recognized place. Mircea Eliade made the same point from another angle: initiation marked a real before and after, a death of the boy and the birth of the man. These systems were not gentle, and not always just; I will come back to what was wrong with them. But they performed a task we have abandoned. Campbell's plain conclusion was that modern society has almost no rituals of this kind left. A boy today crosses into manhood through a driver's license, a graduation, and the gradual accumulation of years. None of it tells him he has become anything. 

In its place we have handed boys a single answer to the question of what a person is worth. The language gives it away: net worth. A boy raised inside this frame is told in a hundred quiet ways that his worth is his future earning power. School becomes credentialing for a wage. Every activity has to justify itself by whether it builds the resume. Wendell Berry, in *The Unsettling of America*, names the underlying logic. He distinguishes two stances toward anything--the exploiter and the nurturer. The exploiter asks how much he can get and how fast; the nurturer asks what condition the thing is in, what it needs, and whether it will remain healthy. The clearest modern form of the exploiter's question is the one we now ask about our own time: what is an hour of it worth? Pricing a life by the hour is that question turned back onto the person living it. And when the labor market has no use for a boy's strength, his attention, or his narrow skill, the metric tells him with perfect clarity that he is worth less. 

Real value lives in the relationship, the care, the competence, and the fidelity that effort serves. Growing tomatoes, making a table, repairing a fence, staying with a hard friendship, cooking for people, sitting with a child who needs you--these can look irrational under a clean economic calculation, and their unreasonableness is precisely the tell that no one is running them through a spreadsheet. Berry is not alone in seeing the reduction: James C. Scott showed why states and markets privilege whatever they can count, so that the most legible measure of a person becomes what he earns; Oliver Burkeman, what happens once hours become a resource to optimize. None of this means economics has no place--the dollars-per-hour measure helped people escape inherited drudgery, and specialization feeds cities that could never feed themselves--but the deformation begins the moment economic value becomes the only register left in the room. The economized frame does more than fail to build a boy's inner world. It tells him the inner world does not count. Which leaves the question it can never answer: if a man's worth is not his wage, where does it come from, and how is it built?

One answer the old cultures embodied is that a man is made, and made in the presence of other men. Robert Bly framed this in *Iron John*. Manhood, in his telling, is not something that arrives on its own with age; it is transmitted, handed from older men to younger ones through attention, example, and initiation. The ritual passage was the visible form of that transmission--the marked separation, the ordeal, the return--but the substance was always the relationship: men deciding a boy was ready, and showing him what readiness meant. This is the structure of value the economic frame cannot see. A boy's worth is not proven in a market. It is recognized, named, and cultivated by people who already carry the thing they are trying to pass on.

When that transmission fails, the boy does not stop developing; he develops wrong. Bly's point was that fathers today are often physically present but emotionally remote or simply gone, and that without something passing from older men to younger ones, certain passages never complete. The boy becomes capable on the surface and unformed underneath. Bly called the result the unfinished man, and he saw the absence producing two recognizable types: the passive man who never quite launches, and the brittle, aggressive man performing a strength he was never actually given.

And the absence is not only of fathers. The wider circle that used to stand around a boy--the uncles, coaches, neighbors, and tradesmen, the whole community of men a father could never replace by himself--has thinned too. Sebastian Junger, in *Tribe*, describes how thoroughly humans are built for interdependent belonging and shared purpose, and how corrosive it is to live in a way that lets everyone get through life without needing anyone. Robert Nisbet made the related argument decades earlier in *The Quest for Community*: as the institutions that once stood between the individual and the wider world weakened--the family, the church, the guild, the town--people did not become serenely self-sufficient. They became lonely, and went searching. What is left where all of that used to be is a vacuum.

A vacuum does not stay empty. A boy with no community and no model will accept both from whoever offers them, and right now the people most eager to offer him a complete account of manhood--a set of rules, a tribe, a verdict on his own worth--are figures like Andrew Tate and the broader manosphere, amplified by feeds engineered to reward grievance and contempt. This is the hinge of the whole problem. Left without anyone to tell him what he is for, a boy does not conclude that the question has no answer. He takes the first answer offered, and the worst men online have made sure theirs is the loudest.

Erich Fromm saw why almost a century ago. In *Escape from Freedom* he argued that freedom, once stripped of belonging, stops feeling like liberation and starts feeling like exposure. The person who has been cut loose from every fixed role and told he may become anything often experiences that openness as a weight he cannot carry alone, and then goes looking for someone or something to relieve him of it. Fromm was describing the rise of authoritarianism in the 1930s. The description applies with uncomfortable precision to a fourteen-year-old boy with a phone and no clear sense of what he is for. The freedom we are so proud of handing him is, without belonging, just exposure--and someone is always waiting to relieve him of it.

Part of why we lose the argument before it starts is that we let the language get away from us, and the only words now widely available for a boy's nature are negative ones. He hears "masculinity," which arrives faintly suspect, or "toxic masculinity," which arrives as an accusation. It is worth knowing where that second phrase came from. It was coined in the 1980s within the mythopoetic men's movement--the same world that produced *Iron John*--by men who were trying to recover and honor masculinity. They used "toxic" to name a specific deformation: shame, emotional shutdown, compulsive dominance, contempt for anything soft. Their point depended on a distinction. A healthy masculinity could be honored; a damaged version needed to be corrected. The phrase assumed there was a good thing under threat. Only later did it harden in popular use into something that sounds like a verdict on men as such. There are genuinely toxic expressions of masculinity, and a boy needs help seeing the difference between them and the real thing. Telling him that his nature is the problem only guarantees he will go find someone who tells him otherwise.

I promised to come back to what was wrong with the old way, and it was a great deal. The past we are tempted to miss was full of its own dangers. The same rites of passage that gave boys a clear place also enforced rigid roles, excluded everyone who did not fit, and carried real cruelty inside them. A world in which older men confidently told younger ones exactly what to do was also a world that told a great many people--women, anyone who did not fit the mold--to stay in a place they never chose. Whatever we recover has to pass through that knowledge. We need to somehow recover the function without the cage.

There is a second honesty I owe the argument, because a story this tidy should make anyone suspicious. The account I have given is a cultural one--meaning, formation, belonging--and there is a competing explanation that Richard Reeves, whose numbers opened this essay, leans toward. On his reading the crisis is largely structural. Boys mature later than girls and sit in a school system poorly built for how they develop. The disappearance of well-paid physical work hit men first and hardest. Fathers have been separated from their children by divorce, distance, and law. His remedies are concrete and secular: start boys in school a year later, recruit far more male teachers, draw men into the growing fields of health, education, and care. Those remedies stand even without the larger story I have told, and the truer picture probably has both kinds of cause feeding each other. The trouble is not boys alone, either--girls are at least as anxious and unhappy by many measures, as Jonathan Haidt documents in *The Anxious Generation*, which suggests the underlying condition reaches all our children. What seems particular to boys is what Reeves calls cultural redundancy: girls were handed a new and expansive account of who they could become, and boys were handed mostly a list of things to avoid.

So, knowing all of that, the answer is still mostly about men. Boys need older men in their lives beyond their fathers--the coaches and uncles and neighbors and tradesmen who make up the wider circle a father cannot supply by himself. They need real responsibility and real competence: work with consequences, work that belongs to a household or a community, the kind of mastery Matthew Crawford writes about in *Shop Class as Soulcraft*, where building and fixing actual things becomes an answer to a drift no screen can cure. They need some marked passage into accountability--a trip, a project, a threshold that says plainly: you were a boy, and now more is expected of you. They need several places in their lives where nobody is calculating the return, because those are the places a self gets built. And they need us to take the feed seriously as a rival for their attention, because every account of manhood we decline to give them leaves more room for an algorithm to provide one.

## Reading List

* *Why Liberalism Failed* -- Patrick Deneen. How a politics built on individual choice erodes the communities that form people.
* *The Power of Myth* -- Joseph Campbell. Initiation and passage as structures by which cultures form their young.
* *The Sacred and the Profane* -- Mircea Eliade. Rites of passage and the difference between a marked life and an unmarked one.
* *The Unsettling of America* -- Wendell Berry. Specialization, membership, ceremony, and useful work as conditions under which people are formed.
* *Seeing Like a State* -- James C. Scott. Why institutions privilege what can be counted, categorized, and made legible.
* *Tools for Conviviality* -- Ivan Illich. How professionalized institutions can rob ordinary people of their own capacities.
* *The Master and His Emissary* -- Iain McGilchrist. The difference between grasping control and relational attention.
* *Four Thousand Weeks* -- Oliver Burkeman. Why treating time as an optimizable resource changes the character of a life.
* *Man's Search for Meaning* -- Viktor Frankl. The human need to be needed, and what its absence costs.
* *Iron John* -- Robert Bly. The absence of male initiation and the unfinished men it produces.
* *Tribe* -- Sebastian Junger. Human belonging, shared purpose, and the loneliness of a society that needs no one.
* *The Quest for Community* -- Robert Nisbet. What happens when the institutions between people and the world dissolve.
* *Escape from Freedom* -- Erich Fromm. Why freedom without belonging becomes a weight, and where men go to set it down.
* *Of Boys and Men* -- Richard Reeves. The data on how boys and men are falling behind, and a structural account of why.
* *The Anxious Generation* -- Jonathan Haidt. The wider crisis of childhood that reaches girls as much as boys.
* *Shop Class as Soulcraft* -- Matthew B. Crawford. Competence with real things as an answer to abstraction and drift.
