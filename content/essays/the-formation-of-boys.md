# The Formation of Boys

As I'm writing this, my boy is 15. It's 2026. I'm nervous, and excited for him. He's awesome. He's into blacksmithing, Muay Thai, math, learning. He's kind and funny. He loves spending time with us, and I love spending time with him. It won't last, he'll change, I'll change, and the world will change. But those changes - who's driving them? What's driving them? This essay is me thnking out loud. Any place it comes across as 'certain' is a side effect of writing. The reality is I have nothing but questions about everything here.

Let's start with some data - numbers from Richard Reeves, president of the American Institute for Boys and Men that gives some data to the feels. Women are substantially more likely to finish college; AIBM puts the four-year graduation rate at four-year institutions at 54 female vs 43 male. Men account for most fatal injury deaths, including roughly seven in ten opioid overdose deaths and nearly four in five suicides. Men commit suicide 4x more than women - and 39,000 do it each year, roughly comparable to deaths from breast cancer. There's tons of endless data, all pointing out that we've got a problem. 

I'm talking boys - but it's all people in modernity. This is a facet of the issues we've been struggling with for the last few hudnred years (thousands?). The modern world atomizes people - it makes them indepedent, cut off, abstract, specialized, disconnected. We're all rootless, disconnected from our bodies, lacking community. Boys are where some of the damage shows early and plainly. It's impacting all of us, but for this essay, let's focus on boys - on my son, on me.

Patrick Deneen, in *Why Liberalism Failed*, is a great opening. A civilization organized around maximizing individual choice will, over time, dissolve the families, communities, and traditions that produced capable individuals in the first place. The unencumbered self, freed from every bond it did not personally choose, eventually finds that the surrounding structures which used to hold a life together have thinned to nothing. Since everything is political - let's call out that Deneen himself has drifted toward a politics that I do not share. The book is still incredible. We have spent a long time treating inherited obligation as a constraint to be removed, and we are living among the consequences of removing it.

What we removed, in the case of boys, was thousands of generations of men around a campfire helping boys become men. For almost all of human history, becoming a man was not left to chance. Cultures carried boys across the gap with deliberate machinery. Joseph Campbell spent his life documenting how societies moved boys through a structured passage--separation from childhood, ordeal, and return as someone with a recognized place. These systems were not gentle, and not always just; I'll write more about that further down. But they performed a task we have abandoned. Modern society has almost no rituals of this kind left. I'm Jewish, and didn't even Bar Mitvah my son. We all just slipped into the day by day. There is no effort, no reward, and no accomplishment. 

Without these rituals helping define a man (or a human) we have a single answer for "what is a person worth"?. Their "net worth". Without gods, money is our god. A boy raised inside this frame is told in a hundred quiet ways that his worth is his future earning power. School becomes credentialing for a wage. Every activity has to justify itself by whether it builds the resume. Wendell Berry, in *The Unsettling of America*, distinguishes two stances, the exploiter and the nurturer. The exploiter asks how much he can get and how fast; the nurturer asks what condition the thing is in, what it needs, and whether it will remain healthy. We've thrown away the nuturer almost entirely, and we're all exploiters. The exploiters rallying question is "what is ym time worth"? Pricing a life by the hour is that question turned back onto the person living it. And when the labor market has no use for a boy's strength, his attention, or his narrow skill, the metric tells him with perfect clarity that he is worth less. 

An aside - the entitlement and messed up sense of values inherent in valuing time as money and value is it's own entire discussion. 20 years ago one of my coworkers was complaining about doing some 'menial' task - maybe filing expenses, or cleaning the office, or fixing something - and pointed out that he was creating so much value for the comapny that every hour on these trivial things was wasting his time. Why couldn't we just hire someone else to do that stuff instead? Why can't we hire someone else to clean our house (which I do?) Or hire someone else to raise our kids (which I did)? Or live our life? The mistake is thinkign that these things aren't 'our job', or 'our life'.

Real value lives in the relationship, the care, the competence, and the fidelity that effort serves. Growing tomatoes, making a table, repairing a fence, staying with a hard friendship, cooking for people, sitting with a child who needs you--these can look irrational under a clean economic calculation. Berry is not alone in seeing the reduction: James C. Scott in *Seeing like a State* showed why states and markets privilege whatever they can count, so that the most legible measure of a person becomes what he earns; Oliver Burkeman in *4000 Weeks*, what happens once hours become a resource to optimize. I'm not saying economics has no place. The dollars-per-hour measure helped people escape inherited drudgery, and specialization feeds cities that could never feed themselves--but the deformation begins the moment economic value becomes the only measure. Which leaves the question: if a man's worth is not his wage, where does it come from, and how is it built?

One answer: a man is made, and made in the presence of other men. That's Robert Bly claim in *Iron John*. Manhood is not something that arrives on its own with age; it is transmitted, handed from older men to younger ones through attention, example, and initiation. The ritual passage was the visible form of that transmission--the marked separation, the ordeal, the return (shout out Joseph Campbell)--but the substance was always the relationship: men deciding a boy was ready, and showing him what readiness meant. 

The wider circle that used to stand around a boy--the uncles, coaches, neighbors, and tradesmen, the whole community of men a father could never replace by himself--has thinned too. Sebastian Junger, in *Tribe*, describes how thoroughly humans are built for interdependent belonging and shared purpose, and how corrosive it is to live in a way that lets everyone get through life without needing anyone. Robert Nisbet made the related argument decades earlier in *The Quest for Community*: as the institutions that once stood between the individual and the wider world weakened--the family, the church, the guild, the town--people did not become serenely self-sufficient. They became lonely, and went searching. What is left where all of that used to be is a vacuum.

A vacuum does not stay empty. A boy with no community and no model will accept both from whoever offers them, and right now the people willing to say "THIS IS WHAT IT MEANS TO BE A MAN" are only the people I don't want saying anythign - Andrew Tatte, the manosphere, looksmaxing, etc. Left without anyone to tell him what he is for, a boy does not decide that the question has no answer. He takes the first answer offered, and the worst people online have made sure theirs is the loudest.

Erich Fromm saw why almost a century ago. In *Escape from Freedom* he argued that freedom, once stripped of belonging, stops feeling like liberation and starts feeling like exposure. The person who has been cut loose from every fixed role and told he may become anything often experiences that openness as a weight he cannot carry alone, and then goes looking for someone or something to relieve him of it. Fromm was describing the rise of authoritarianism in the 1930s. The description applies with uncomfortable precision to a fourteen-year-old boy with a phone and no clear sense of what he is for. 

Part of why we lose the argument before it starts is that we let the language get away from us, and the only words now widely available for a boy's nature are negative ones. He hears "masculinity," which arrives faintly suspect, or "toxic masculinity," which arrives as an accusation. It is worth knowing where that second phrase came from. It was coined in the 1980s within the mythopoetic men's movement--the same world that produced *Iron John*--by men who were trying to recover and honor masculinity. They used "toxic" to name a specific deformation: shame, emotional shutdown, compulsive dominance, contempt for anything soft. Their point depended on a distinction. A healthy masculinity could be honored; a damaged version needed to be corrected. The phrase assumed there was a good thing under threat. Only later did it harden in popular use into something that sounds like a verdict on men as such. There are genuinely toxic expressions of masculinity, and a boy needs help seeing the difference between them and the real thing. Telling him that his nature is the problem only guarantees he will go find someone who tells him otherwise.

I'm not romantisizing (too much) the past. The same rites of passage that gave boys a clear place also enforced rigid roles, excluded everyone who did not fit, and carried real cruelty. A world in which older men confidently told younger ones exactly what to do was also a world that told a great many people--women, anyone who did not fit the mold--to stay in a place they never chose. Whatever we recover has to pass through that knowledge. We need to somehow recover the function without the cage.

Boys need older men in their lives beyond their fathers--the coaches and uncles and neighbors and tradesmen who make up the wider circle a father cannot supply by himself. They need real responsibility and real competence: work with consequences, work that belongs to a household or a community, the kind of mastery Matthew Crawford writes about in *Shop Class as Soulcraft*, where building and fixing actual things becomes an answer to a drift no screen can cure. They need some marked passage into accountability--a trip, a project, a threshold that says plainly: you were a boy, and now more is expected of you. They need several places in their lives where nobody is calculating the return, because those are the places a self gets built. And they need us to take the feed seriously as a rival for their attention, because every account of manhood we decline to give them leaves more room for an algorithm to provide one.

## Reading List

* *Why Liberalism Failed* -- Patrick Deneen. How a politics built on individual choice erodes the communities that form people.
* *The Power of Myth* -- Joseph Campbell. Initiation and passage as structures by which cultures form their young.
* *The Unsettling of America* -- Wendell Berry. Specialization, membership, ceremony, and useful work as conditions under which people are formed.
* *Seeing Like a State* -- James C. Scott. Why institutions privilege what can be counted, categorized, and made legible.
* *Four Thousand Weeks* -- Oliver Burkeman. Why treating time as an optimizable resource changes the character of a life.
* *Iron John* -- Robert Bly. The absence of male initiation and the unfinished men it produces.
* *Tribe* -- Sebastian Junger. Human belonging, shared purpose, and the loneliness of a society that needs no one.
* *The Quest for Community* -- Robert Nisbet. What happens when the institutions between people and the world dissolve.
* *Escape from Freedom* -- Erich Fromm. Why freedom without belonging becomes a weight, and where men go to set it down.
* *Of Boys and Men* -- Richard Reeves. The data on how boys and men are falling behind, and a structural account of why.
* *The Anxious Generation* -- Jonathan Haidt. The wider crisis of childhood that reaches girls as much as boys.
* *Shop Class as Soulcraft* -- Matthew B. Crawford. Competence with real things as an answer to abstraction and drift.
